Guiding injured tissues toward functional regeneration

From repair signals to tissue function

Healing depends on the tissue receiving the signal, its inflammatory state, and the forces acting on it. I study how these conditions shape immune and stromal cell behavior, extracellular matrix organization, and the mechanical function of repairing tissues.

My work connects regenerative immunotherapies with engineered biomaterials and controlled tissue models. Following these systems over time helps distinguish a transient cellular response from remodeling that restores useful tissue function.

Immunomechanically Tuned Vascular Grafts to Mitigate Diabetic Inflammation

My NIH/NHLBI K99/R00 Pathway to Independence Award supports research integrating immunoengineering, vascular biomechanics, and disease-relevant models. I · Repair-fate biology

Immune signals in context

I investigate how immune programs interact with stromal cells and tissue state to shape repair. My doctoral work developed helminth-derived immunotherapies that reduced fibrosis and improved functional repair across skeletal muscle, cartilage, and corneal injury models. These studies inform my interest in identifying when an immune intervention supports regeneration and when it contributes to pathological remodeling.

Related study: proregenerative immunotherapies

II · Biomaterial immunomechanics

Connecting material design to host remodeling

Vascular grafts provide a demanding test of functional tissue regeneration: the remodeled tissue must integrate with the host while sustaining mechanical loading. I combine biomaterial design, immune characterization, matrix analysis, and biomechanical testing to investigate how inflammatory environments and material properties shape that process.

Collaborative work on sex-specific graft remodeling and immune–stromal networks in the foreign body response extends this question across biological contexts.

Related work on vascular graft remodeling

III · Experimental platforms

Following remodeling as it unfolds

I developed an organotypic artery–graft culture platform that preserves the tissue–material interface and supports repeated, non-destructive multiphoton imaging. Combined with quantitative collagen analysis and controlled perturbations, it allows remodeling to be examined over time and compared with selected features of long-term graft explants.

These systems connect cellular and matrix changes with mechanics-informed readouts, helping identify mechanisms to test in more complex injury and disease models.

Long-term direction

Control of tissue repair fate

My goal is to define how immune signals, stromal states, and mechanical conditions can be changed to guide injured tissues toward durable, functional regeneration. Vascular grafts and organotypic models provide experimental systems for building this broader research program.
